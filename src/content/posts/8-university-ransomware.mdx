When people think about ransomware attacks, they might imagine hackers breaking through complicated security systems using advanced tools and sophisticated code.

But sometimes, getting into a system can be much simpler than that.

Sometimes, all an attacker needs is a password.

I came across a **Sophos report published on August 27, 2026** about ransomware in education, and one statistic immediately stood out to me: **identity-based attacks were involved in 85% of ransomware attacks against educational institutions.** The report also found that malicious email was the leading technical root cause of these attacks.

You can read the original report from Sophos here: [Identity-Based Attacks Are Responsible for 85% of Ransomware in Education](https://www.sophos.com/en-us/press/2026/08/identity-based-attacks-are-responsible-for-85-percent-of-ransomware-in-education).

That statistic made me think about how important something as simple as a university password can be.

This highlights an important cybersecurity lesson: sometimes the biggest weakness in a system isn't the technology itself. It's access to the people using it.

## Why are universities attractive targets?

Universities are incredibly large and complex organizations.

Think about everything connected to a university network:

* Student accounts
* Faculty and staff accounts
* Financial information
* Research data
* Academic records
* Learning management systems
* Campus Wi-Fi
* Thousands of personal devices

That's a lot of information and a lot of potential entry points.

Universities also store valuable personal, financial, and research information, making them attractive targets for cybercriminals.

Unlike many smaller organizations, universities have thousands of people accessing their systems every day. Students, professors, researchers, staff members, and visitors may all interact with the same digital infrastructure.

The more users there are, the more opportunities attackers have to find a mistake.

## The password problem

Imagine receiving an email that looks like it came from your university.

It might say:

> **Your account will be disabled unless you verify your information immediately.**

The email looks official.

It uses the university's logo. Maybe it even looks similar to emails you've received before.

You click the link.

You're taken to a page that looks exactly like your university's login page.

You enter your username and password.

But instead of logging into your university account, you've just given your credentials directly to an attacker.

This is one of the reasons phishing remains so dangerous.

According to the Sophos report, malicious email was the leading technical root cause of ransomware attacks in both lower and higher education. It accounted for 31% of attacks in lower education and 29% in higher education.

Attackers don't always need to break through complicated technical defenses. Sometimes, they can convince someone to voluntarily give them access.

## From one account to an entire network

Stealing a password doesn't always mean an attacker immediately has access to everything.

However, a compromised account can become the starting point for something much bigger.

Once attackers gain access, they may try to:

* Access sensitive files
* Search for additional credentials
* Move through the network
* Gain higher levels of access
* Disable security tools
* Deploy ransomware

This is why identity security has become such an important part of cybersecurity.

Attackers don't always need to find a complicated vulnerability in software.

Sometimes, they can simply **log in**.

And once an attacker has access to a legitimate account, detecting malicious activity can become much more difficult.

## The impact of ransomware on universities

A ransomware attack can affect much more than just computers.

Universities depend on technology for almost everything, including:

* Registering for classes
* Accessing assignments
* Communicating with professors
* Managing financial systems
* Conducting research
* Storing academic records

When ransomware disrupts these systems, the entire university can feel the effects.

Students may lose access to important systems. Classes and assignments can be disrupted. Faculty and staff may be unable to access the tools they rely on every day.

Research can also be affected, especially when universities store years of valuable academic and scientific work.

Recovering from a ransomware attack can take time, money, and significant effort.

The Sophos report found that the average ransomware recovery cost for education organizations reached **$2.26 million**. It also found that 26% of education organizations required between one and three months to fully recover from an attack.

The consequences can continue long after the initial attack.

## What can students do?

Students might not control a university's cybersecurity systems, but they can still play an important role in protecting their accounts.

Here are a few simple things that can make a difference.

### Use strong and unique passwords

Avoid using the same password across multiple accounts.

If one website experiences a data breach, attackers may try those same credentials on other websites and services.

Using strong and unique passwords makes it more difficult for one compromised account to affect multiple accounts.

A password manager can also help generate and store secure passwords.

### Be suspicious of urgent emails

Attackers often try to create a sense of urgency.

Be careful with messages that say things like:

* "Your account will be deleted."
* "Verify your account immediately."
* "Click here to avoid suspension."

Instead of clicking a link directly in an unexpected email, go to the official university website and log in from there.

### Enable multi-factor authentication

Multi-factor authentication adds another layer of security to an account.

Even if someone steals your password, they may still need access to your phone, authentication app, or another verification method.

Your password alone is no longer the only thing protecting your account.

### Think before you click

Phishing attacks often depend on people acting quickly.

Taking a few extra seconds to check the sender, inspect a link, and question an unexpected message can help prevent an attack.

If something feels suspicious, it's better to pause and verify it before clicking.

## Cybersecurity isn't just about technology

The biggest takeaway from the Sophos report is that cybersecurity isn't only about firewalls, antivirus software, and complicated code.

It's also about people.

A password is more than just a way to log into an account.

It can be a key.

And if that key ends up in the wrong hands, it could potentially open the door to an entire organization.

For universities with thousands of students and employees, protecting identities has become just as important as protecting the network itself.

So the next time you receive an unexpected email asking you to log in, remember:

**Your password could be the front door.**

Make sure you're giving the key to the right person.
