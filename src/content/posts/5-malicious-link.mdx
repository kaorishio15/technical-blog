We've all been told not to click suspicious links.

But what actually happens if you do?

Before learning more about cybersecurity, I thought clicking one bad link meant your computer was immediately hacked. The reality is a little more complicated.

## What makes a link malicious?

A link itself isn't necessarily dangerous.

The important part is where the link takes you and what happens after you get there.

A malicious link could lead to:

- **A fake login page** designed to steal your credentials
- **A malicious download** containing malware
- **An exploit** that attempts to take advantage of a software vulnerability
- **A fraudulent website** designed to collect personal information
- **A scam page** that tries to convince you to give away sensitive information

This is one reason phishing is such an important cybersecurity topic.

## The fake login page

One of the easiest ways to understand a malicious link is to imagine receiving an email from a service you use.

The email looks legitimate and tells you that there is a problem with your account.

You click the link.

The website looks almost identical to the real website, so you enter your username and password.

The page might then show an error message or redirect you somewhere else.

From your perspective, nothing major happened.

But your credentials may have just been sent to an attacker.

The attacker can then try using those credentials to access the real account.

## What about malware?

A malicious link can also lead to a download.

The downloaded file could contain malware that attempts to run on your device.

There are many different types of malware, including programs designed to steal information, monitor activity, damage files, or give an attacker access to a system.

However, simply clicking a link does not automatically mean malware has been installed.

What happens depends on the website, the file, the device, the software being used, and whether additional security protections stop the attack.

## Why are malicious links effective?

The technical part of these attacks isn't always the most interesting part.

Often, the attacker first needs to convince someone to click.

This is where social engineering comes into play.

A message might create a sense of urgency or make the person think they are responding to something important.

For example:

- "Your account will be locked today."
- "There is suspicious activity on your account."
- "Your payment could not be processed."
- "Please review this document."

The goal is to make the person act before they stop and think about whether the message is legitimate.

## What should you do if you click one?

If you accidentally click a suspicious link, the first thing to do is not panic.

What you should do depends on what happened.

If you didn't enter any information or download anything, the risk may be lower.

If you entered a password, changing that password and enabling MFA can help protect the account.

If you downloaded or ran something suspicious, contacting the appropriate IT or security team is a good idea.

The important thing is to report potential incidents instead of assuming that nothing happened.

## What I'm taking away

One of the biggest things I'm learning about cybersecurity is that security usually works through layers.

Someone might accidentally click a phishing link.

Email security might block the message.

The browser might warn about the website.

MFA might prevent the stolen password from being enough.

Endpoint security might detect a malicious file.

There are multiple opportunities to stop an attack.

The goal isn't to assume that nobody will ever make a mistake.

It's to make sure that **one mistake doesn't automatically become a major security incident**.
