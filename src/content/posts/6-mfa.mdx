One of the first cybersecurity recommendations I remember hearing is:

**Turn on MFA.**

And honestly, it's good advice.

But the more I've learned about cybersecurity, the more I've realized that MFA isn't a magic shield that makes an account impossible to hack.

## What is MFA?

Multi-factor authentication means using more than one authentication factor to verify your identity.

These factors are commonly grouped into three categories:

- **Something you know** — such as a password
- **Something you have** — such as a phone or security key
- **Something you are** — such as a fingerprint

The idea is that an attacker should need more than one piece of information to access an account.

## Why is MFA useful?

Imagine that someone gets your password through a phishing attack.

Without MFA, that password might be enough to log into your account.

With MFA enabled, the attacker may also need another authentication factor.

NIST recommends MFA because it can protect accounts even when passwords have been compromised.

That's a pretty big security improvement.

## So what's the problem?

Not all MFA methods provide the same level of protection.

Some attackers can try to trick users into giving them one-time codes.

For example, an attacker could create a fake login page and ask for:

1. Your username
2. Your password
3. Your MFA code

The attacker could then try using that information against the real service.

This is why phishing-resistant authentication is becoming increasingly important.

## What is phishing-resistant MFA?

Phishing-resistant authentication is designed to prevent an attacker from simply collecting authentication information through a fake website.

Security keys and some other cryptographic authentication methods can provide stronger protection against phishing.

Passkeys are another example of authentication technology designed with phishing resistance in mind.

## Does that mean regular MFA is useless?

Definitely not.

MFA is still much better than relying only on a password.

The important thing is understanding that cybersecurity is about **reducing risk**, not eliminating every possible risk.

Adding another layer makes an attack harder.

Using a stronger authentication method can make it even harder.

## What I'm taking away

Before learning about cybersecurity, I probably would have said:

"I have MFA, so I'm safe."

Now I'd phrase it differently:

"I have MFA, so my account has another layer of protection."

That might sound like a small difference, but I think it represents a much better way of thinking about cybersecurity.

No single security control solves every problem.

Good security comes from combining multiple layers.
