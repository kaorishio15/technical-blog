I am currently taking the class Cybersecurity Risk **CSCE 447 at Texas A&M University**, and a topic in today's class that stood out to me was the scale and cost of data breaches.
I know what data breaches are, how they can happen, and their impacts, but I never thought about the real impact in terms of costs in number before.

## The numbers surprised me

The United States has experienced an enormous number of data breaches over the years. According to the Identity Theft Resource Center, publicly reported U.S. data compromises have resulted in nearly **12 billion victim notices** and approximately **60 billion exposed records** since tracking began in 2005.

The U.S. also ranked first globally for compromised accounts in 2025, with approximately **142.9 million accounts affected**.

I was honestly surprised to see the United States rank first. I expected the numbers to be high, but seeing the U.S. at the top of the list made the scale of the problem feel much more real.

And the impact isn't only measured by how many accounts or records are exposed.

According to recent reporting on IBM's data breach research, the average cost of a data breach in the United States reached **$10.22 million**, compared with a global average of about **$4.44 million**.

That's a huge difference.

## So what does this have to do with cybersecurity?

This is where cybersecurity risk started to make more sense to me.

A data breach isn't just a technical problem. It can become a **financial, operational, and reputational problem** as well.

Organizations have to consider questions like:

- What information are we storing?
- Who has access to it?
- What happens if that information is stolen?
- How quickly can we detect an attack?
- How much would it cost to recover?

Cybersecurity is therefore not just about preventing attacks. It's also about **managing risk** and preparing for what happens when something goes wrong.

## What I'm taking away

One thing I'm learning is that cybersecurity isn't something that can be treated as an afterthought.

The amount of information organizations collect continues to grow, and protecting that information requires more than just having a firewall or antivirus software.

It involves understanding the risks, protecting sensitive data, limiting unnecessary access, monitoring systems, and having a plan for responding to incidents.

Learning about these numbers in CSCE 447 gave me a different perspective on cybersecurity. I'm starting to see why security decisions aren't only technical decisions—they can have a major impact on an entire organization.

And that's something I want to keep learning more about.
