When I first started learning about cybersecurity, I thought phishing was mostly about obviously fake emails with strange links and spelling mistakes.

The more I learn about cybersecurity, the more I realize that phishing can be much more convincing than that.

## What is phishing?

Phishing is a type of social engineering where someone tries to trick another person into giving up information or taking an action that benefits an attacker.

This could mean giving away a password, clicking a malicious link, downloading a file, or providing other sensitive information.

Phishing can happen through many different forms of communication, including email, text messages, social media, and phone calls.

Some common examples include:

- **Email phishing** — sending fraudulent emails that appear to come from a legitimate organization
- **Spear phishing** — creating a targeted message for a specific person or organization
- **Smishing** — phishing through text messages
- **Vishing** — phishing through phone calls or voice communication
- **Malicious links** — sending someone to a fake or harmful website

## Why does phishing work?

One of the things I find interesting about phishing is that it isn't always about exploiting a technical vulnerability.

Instead, attackers can take advantage of normal human behavior.

People tend to trust messages that appear to come from organizations they recognize. They also tend to react quickly when something seems urgent.

A phishing message might say that an account is about to be locked, that there was suspicious activity, or that a payment needs to be confirmed.

The goal is to make the person react before they have time to stop and question the message.

## It isn't always obvious

I think one of the biggest misconceptions about phishing is that it should be easy to recognize.

Sometimes it is.

But phishing messages don't always look like obvious scams.

They can use familiar logos, professional-looking language, realistic websites, and information about the person being targeted.

That makes it important to look at what the message is actually asking you to do.

Was I expecting this message?

Is it asking me to log in?

Is someone asking for sensitive information?

Does the request seem unusually urgent?

Those questions can sometimes be more useful than simply looking for spelling mistakes.

## How can phishing be prevented?

There isn't one security tool that completely prevents phishing.

Organizations can use email filtering and other security technologies to detect suspicious messages. They can also train employees and students to recognize common phishing techniques.

MFA can provide another layer of protection as well. If an attacker manages to steal a password through phishing, having another authentication factor can make it harder for them to access the account.

Some newer authentication methods, such as passkeys and security keys, are also designed to be more resistant to phishing.

## What I'm taking away

Phishing is a good example of why cybersecurity isn't only about computers and software.

An organization can have strong security controls, but people are still interacting with emails, websites, messages, and applications.

That means cybersecurity has to consider both **technology and human behavior**.

The more I learn about cybersecurity, the more I realize that sometimes an attacker doesn't need to break into a system.

Sometimes they just need to convince someone to let them in.
